Paper Map

Paper 1 Reading is 20% of the subject. You have 1½ hours for Parts A and B together.

Part A (Compulsory) — 10%

  • Every candidate sits Part A.
  • One or more texts; topics and genres vary.
  • Easier and harder items mixed: MC, short answers, open-ended.

Part B — choose B1 or B2 (each 10%)

B1 (easier) B2 (more difficult)
Texts More straightforward More challenging
Demand Less cognitively demanding More cognitively demanding
Highest level Level 4 (A + B1) Full range, including Level 5 / 5** (A + B2)

Part B tests the same skills as Part A — at a different level.

Timing

  • 1½ hours covers A + your chosen B.
  • Leave a clear block for B; check answers at the end.
  • Choose B1 or B2 with your teacher before exam day.

Item types

  • gap-fill / cloze · True / False / Not Given · matching · referencing
  • multiple choice · chart / table · ordering · open-ended

Reading Skills

Use these skills on every text. Later pages will show how each question type uses them.

Skimming

  • Read the title, subtitles, first and last paragraphs, and topic sentences.
  • Ask: What is this about? Who is it for? What is the writer doing?

First pass = a map. Do not stop for every unknown word.

Example

A Lion Rock hiking feature — skim once to see it is about safety and trail etiquette, not a history of the peak.

Scanning

  • Know the keyword (name, date, number, place) before you search.
  • Hunt that word or a clear synonym; ignore the rest until you hit the line.

Check the sentence answers the question — same word, wrong context, no mark.

Example

Question asks for the MTR exit for a Saturday market → scan for exit, station name, or Saturday, then read that sentence only.

Main Idea

  • Prefer the topic sentence (often first or last) over colourful examples.
  • The answer covers the whole paragraph or text, not one supporting detail.

MC distractors often reuse exact words from a side example.

Example

Three reasons students join a cha chaan teng part-time job (pay, hours, language practice) → main idea = why the job attracts students, not the price of milk tea.

Detail

  • Match question keywords to the right paragraph; answers are often short.
  • Watch synonyms: the question may say teenagers when the text says Form 4 students.

Copy carefully when told to use the text — spelling can change meaning.

Example

“Name ONE benefit of the school’s recycling scheme in paragraph 3” → lift the benefit named there, not a benefit from paragraph 5.

Inferring

  • The answer is suggested, not spelled out. Clues: imply, suggest, most likely, why…?
  • Stay inside the text — do not invent ideas from general knowledge alone.

Use your own words when asked to explain; a bare quote is rarely enough.

Example

A parent says the typhoon signal “saved” their picnic plans by cancelling school → infer relief / gratitude, not that they love typhoons.

Vocabulary in Context

  • Read the sentence before and after; writers often explain nearby.
  • Check part of speech, tense, and singular/plural so the synonym fits.

For MC meaning questions, swap in each option and test the paragraph’s tone.

Example

In a review, “The queue outside the egg waffle stall was brutal” → brutal here means very tough / severe, not “violent crime”.

Tone / Attitude

  • Tone = how the writer sounds; attitude = how they stand toward the topic.
  • Prefer precise labels (sceptical, approving, ironic) over vague positive / negative.

Evidence is word choice, examples, and what is praised or criticised.

Example

A columnist calls a new harbourfront path “long overdue” and “a gift to walkers” → attitude is approving / supportive.

Views / Opinions

  • Separate facts from someone’s view (writer, interviewee, critic).
  • For Yes/No + evidence, pick a stance you can support, then cite it.

When two sides are asked for, both reasons must be in the passage.

Example

A shop owner welcomes more tourists; a resident worries about noise → two views, both text-based.

Cohesion / Reference

  • Pronouns (this, that, they, it) and linkers (however, meanwhile) point to ideas.
  • Read the sentence before the reference word; check gender and singular/plural.

Answer with a clear noun phrase — not a whole sentence unless asked.

Example

“Many residents opposed the plan. This delayed the vote.” → This = residents’ opposition to the plan.

Exam Habits

Adapted from HKEAA briefing / seminar recommendations for Paper 1:

  • Choose B1 or B2 to match your ability (with teacher advice before the exam). Aiming for Level 5 normally means attempting B2; B1 caps the highest attainable level at 4.
  • Read every instruction — word limits, “ONE word”, “from paragraph 4”, “use a phrase from the text”.
  • When told to use words from the text, comply — but do not dump whole sentences into every blank.
  • Avoid over-copying on open-ended items; answer the question, then stop. Extra irrelevant detail can hurt clarity.
  • For gap-fill / cloze, check meaning and word form (tense, singular/plural, part of speech). Re-read the completed paragraph.
  • In summary tasks, use both the original and the summary so you do not repeat information the summary already gives.
  • Watch spelling and punctuation where a change would create a different meaning; include all required elements (e.g. TWO examples = two).
  • Manage the 1½ hours across A and B: finish with time to check unanswered items and careless slips.
