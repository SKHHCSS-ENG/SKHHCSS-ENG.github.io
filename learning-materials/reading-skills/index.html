Paper 1 - Reading

HKDSE English Language · study notes (overview)

Paper Map

Weighting and time (HKEAA): Paper 1 Reading is 20% of the subject. You have 1½ hours for Parts A and B together.

Part A (Compulsory) — 10%

  • Every candidate attempts Part A.
  • One or more texts (about 900–1200 words across the paper parts); topics and genres vary.
  • Tests a range of reading skills — easier and harder items appear together.
  • Typical response formats: multiple choice, short answers, open-ended questions.

Part B — choose B1 or B2 (each 10%)

B1 (easier) B2 (more difficult)
Texts More straightforward More challenging
Demand Less cognitively demanding More cognitively demanding
Highest level Level 4 (A + B1) Full range, including Level 5 / 5** (A + B2)

What Part B tests: the same broad reading skills as Part A (purpose and meaning, main ideas and details, vocabulary in context, tone/mood, views and arguments, language features, selecting and organising information) — but at different difficulty levels.

Rough timing tips (from the paper design, not minute-by-minute rules)

  • The clock covers A + your chosen B in one sitting (1½ hours).
  • Do not “spend the whole paper on A”: leave a clear block for B, then use any remaining minutes to check answers and spelling.
  • Decide B1 or B2 before exam day with your teacher’s advice — not in the exam room under panic.

Reading Skills

Use these skills on every text. Later pages will show how each question type uses them.

Skimming

  • Read title, subtitles, first and last paragraphs, and topic sentences for a quick “map” of the text.
  • Ask: What is this mainly about? Who is it for? What is the writer trying to do?
  • Do not stop for every unknown word on the first pass.
  • Mini example: A feature on weekend hiking at Lion Rock — skim once to see it is about safety and trail etiquette, not a history of the peak.

Scanning

  • Know the keyword (name, date, number, place) before you search.
  • Move your eyes for that word or a clear synonym; ignore the rest until you hit the right line.
  • Confirm the sentence actually answers the question — same word in a different context does not count.
  • Mini example: Question asks for the MTR exit for a Saturday market → scan for exit, station name, or Saturday, then read that sentence only.

Main Idea

  • Prefer the topic sentence (often first or last in a paragraph) over colourful examples.
  • A correct main-idea answer covers the whole paragraph/text, not one supporting detail.
  • In MC options, watch for distractors that reuse exact words from a side example.
  • Mini example: A paragraph lists three reasons students join a cha chaan teng part-time job (pay, hours, language practice) → main idea = why the job attracts students, not the price of milk tea.

Detail

  • Match question keywords to the right paragraph; answers are often short words or phrases.
  • Watch synonyms: the question may say teenagers when the text says Form 4 students.
  • Copy carefully when the instruction allows words from the text — spelling and punctuation can change meaning.
  • Mini example: “Name ONE benefit of the school’s recycling scheme in paragraph 3” → lift the benefit named there, not a benefit from paragraph 5.

Inferring

  • The answer is suggested, not spelled out. Clue words: imply, suggest, most likely, probably, why…?
  • Stay inside the text — do not invent ideas from general knowledge alone.
  • Use your own words when the question asks you to explain; a bare quote is rarely enough.
  • Mini example: A parent says the typhoon signal “saved” their picnic plans by cancelling school → you infer relief / gratitude, not that they love typhoons.

Vocabulary in Context

  • Read the sentence before and after the target word; writers often explain nearby.
  • Check part of speech, tense, and singular/plural so your synonym fits the gap.
  • For MC meaning questions, replace the word with each option and test which fits the paragraph’s tone.
  • Mini example: In a review, “The queue outside the egg waffle stall was brutal” → brutal here means very tough / severe, not “violent crime”.

Tone / Attitude

  • Tone = how the writer sounds; attitude = how they stand toward the topic.
  • Prefer precise labels (sceptical, approving, ironic) over vague positive / negative when the mark scheme wants a specific word.
  • Evidence comes from word choice, examples, and what is praised or criticised.
  • Mini example: A columnist calls a new harbourfront path “long overdue” and “a gift to walkers” → attitude is approving / supportive.

Views / Opinions

  • Separate facts from someone’s view (writer, interviewee, critic).
  • For “Yes/No + evidence” items, pick the stance you can support from the text, then cite that evidence.
  • When two sides are asked for, both reasons must appear in the passage.
  • Mini example: An article quotes a shop owner who welcomes more tourists and a resident who worries about noise → two different views, both text-based.

Cohesion / Reference

  • Pronouns (this, that, they, it) and linkers (however, meanwhile) point back (or sometimes forward) to ideas.
  • Read the sentence before the reference word; check gender and singular/plural.
  • Answer with a clear noun phrase — do not paste a whole sentence unless asked.
  • Mini example: “Many residents opposed the plan. This delayed the vote.” → This = residents’ opposition / their opposition to the plan.

Exam Habits

Adapted from HKEAA briefing / seminar recommendations for Paper 1:

  • Choose B1 or B2 to match your ability (with teacher advice before the exam). Aiming for Level 5 normally means attempting B2; B1 caps the highest attainable level at 4.
  • Read every instruction — word limits, “ONE word”, “from paragraph 4”, “use a phrase from the text”.
  • When told to use words from the text, comply — but do not dump whole sentences into every blank.
  • Avoid over-copying on open-ended items; answer the question, then stop. Extra irrelevant detail can hurt clarity.
  • For gap-fill / cloze, check meaning and word form (tense, singular/plural, part of speech). Re-read the completed paragraph.
  • In summary tasks, use both the original and the summary so you do not repeat information the summary already gives.
  • Watch spelling and punctuation where a change would create a different meaning; include all required elements (e.g. TWO examples = two).
  • Manage the 1½ hours across A and B: finish with time to check unanswered items and careless slips.
