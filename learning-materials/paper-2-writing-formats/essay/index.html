P2 · Part B

Essay

Argue or discuss a view with reasons. Formal — not a letter, story, or magazine article.

P2 / P3B: P2 mainly (Part B). Discuss or argue; invent reasons and examples. Not a letter, blog, or magazine article unless the task says so.

For a closer match to the task: One-sided Argumentative · Two-sided Argumentative · Discursive
PAT
PurposeArgue a stance, or discuss a topic with clear reasons — not to tell a story or write for a named magazine.
AudienceA general, thoughtful reader (examiner / educated public). No named editor or school principal unless the task says so.
ToneFormal and calm. Avoid slang, chatty asides, and letter greetings.
Structure

Opening

  • A short title if the task asks for one
  • Name the issue in one or two sentences
  • End with a clear thesis (your stance, or the two sides you will discuss)

Body

  • 3 paragraphs is usually enough
  • First sentence of each = topic sentence (one main point)
  • Then reason + example
  • If you must argue: one short counter-argument, then return to your view
  • If you must discuss: give both sides fairly, then a reasoned view

Closing

  • Restate the thesis in fresh words
  • End with a final thought or a realistic next step
  • No Dear… / Yours…, and no “thank you for reading”
Skeleton

Slots: [thesis / stance] · [topic / point] · [detail / example] · [other]

Opening

[Title]
In recent years, [issue] has attracted a great deal of attention.
[Thesis — your stance, or the two sides you will discuss].

Body

First, [main point 1]. This is because [reason]. For example, [example].
Second, [main point 2]. If [situation], people can [result].
Some people argue that [counter-view]. However, [your reply].

Closing

In conclusion, [restate stance].
It is therefore worth [final thought / realistic next step].
Useful Phrases
  • It is often argued that…
  • I firmly believe that…
  • One major reason is that…
  • A clear example of this is…
  • Critics may claim that… However,…
  • On the other hand, it is also true that…
  • In conclusion, it is clear that…
  • A practical step would be to…

One-sided Argumentative

Take one clear stand. All main points support your view — not a letter or a magazine article.

PAT
PurposePersuade the reader of one stance. All main points support that view.
AudienceA general, thoughtful reader (examiner / educated public). No named editor or school principal unless the task says so.
ToneFormal, calm, and confident. Avoid slang, chatty asides, and letter greetings.
Structure

Opening

  • A short title if the task asks for one
  • Name the issue in one or two sentences
  • End with a clear thesis (your stance only)

Body

  • 3 paragraphs is usually enough — each one supports your stance
  • First sentence of each = topic sentence (one main point)
  • Then reason + example
  • You may mention the other side in one short sentence, but do not develop it or let it take over a paragraph

Closing

  • Restate the thesis in fresh words
  • End with a final thought or a realistic next step
  • No Dear… / Yours…, and no “thank you for reading”
Skeleton

Slots: [thesis / stance] · [topic / point] · [detail / example] · [other]

Opening

[Title]
In recent years, [issue] has attracted a great deal of attention.
This essay will argue that [thesis — your stance].

Body

First, [main point 1]. This is because [reason]. For example, [example].
Second, [main point 2]. If [situation], people can [result].
Most importantly, [main point 3]. This shows that [link back to stance].

Closing

In conclusion, [restate stance].
It is therefore worth [final thought / realistic next step].
Useful Phrases
  • I firmly believe that…
  • There is no doubt that…
  • The most important reason is that…
  • A clear example of this is…
  • This clearly shows that…
  • It is therefore reasonable to conclude that…
  • A practical step would be to…
  • For these reasons, I would argue that…

Two-sided Argumentative

Argue one stand, and deal with the other side — a short counter-view, then your rebuttal.

PAT
PurposePersuade the reader of one stance, after fairly stating and answering the other side.
AudienceA general, thoughtful reader (examiner / educated public). No named editor or school principal unless the task says so.
ToneFormal, calm, and fair — but still persuasive. Do not sit on the fence.
Structure

Opening

  • A short title if the task asks for one
  • Name the issue in one or two sentences
  • End with a clear thesis (your stance — not only “I will discuss both sides”)

Body

  • 3 paragraphs is usually enough
  • One or two paragraphs for your main reasons: topic sentence + reason + example
  • One paragraph for the other side: state the counter-argument, then rebut it
  • The rebuttal should bring the reader back to your stance

Closing

  • Restate your stance in fresh words (now stronger after the rebuttal)
  • End with a final thought or a realistic next step
  • No Dear… / Yours…, and no “thank you for reading”
Skeleton

Slots: [thesis / stance] · [topic / point] · [detail / example] · [other]

Opening

[Title]
In recent years, [issue] has attracted a great deal of attention.
Although some people argue that [counter-view in brief], I believe that [thesis — your stance].

Body

First, [main point 1]. This is because [reason]. For example, [example].
Second, [main point 2]. If [situation], people can [result].
Some people argue that [counter-view]. However, [rebuttal]. This means that [link back to stance].

Closing

In conclusion, [restate stance].
It is therefore worth [final thought / realistic next step].
Useful Phrases
  • I would argue that…
  • One strong reason is that…
  • A clear example of this is…
  • It is often claimed that… However,…
  • Critics may argue that… In fact,…
  • This objection is not as strong as it seems, because…
  • On balance, the stronger case is that…
  • In conclusion, I still believe that…

Discursive

Explore both sides or several aspects fairly, then offer a reasoned conclusion — more of a discussion than a debate.

PAT
PurposeExplore a topic from more than one angle, and weigh the ideas fairly.
AudienceA general, thoughtful reader (examiner / educated public). No named editor or school principal unless the task says so.
ToneFormal, balanced, and thoughtful. Less like a debate; more like a careful discussion.
Structure

Opening

  • A short title if the task asks for one
  • Name the issue and why it is worth discussing
  • End with a scope line (the sides or aspects you will examine) — not a hard “I firmly believe” thesis, unless the task asks for a stand

Body

  • Give each side or aspect fair space (usually 2–3 paragraphs)
  • First sentence of each = topic sentence (one aspect or one side)
  • Then reason + example
  • Explain and weigh; do not use the other side only as a target to attack

Closing

  • Sum up the main points fairly
  • Offer a reasoned conclusion (a qualified view is fine: “on balance…”)
  • No Dear… / Yours…, and no “thank you for reading”
Skeleton

Slots: [thesis / stance] · [topic / point] · [detail / example] · [other]

Opening

[Title]
In recent years, [issue] has become a matter of public concern.
This essay will discuss [aspect / both sides], before drawing a reasoned conclusion.

Body

On the one hand, [view or aspect 1]. This is because [reason]. For example, [example].
On the other hand, [view or aspect 2]. If [situation], [result].
A further point to consider is [aspect 3]. This suggests that [balanced observation].

Closing

In conclusion, both [side A] and [side B] have some force.
On balance, it seems that [reasoned conclusion / qualified view].
Useful Phrases
  • It is worth considering both sides of this issue.
  • On the one hand,…
  • On the other hand,…
  • Another aspect to consider is…
  • This suggests that…
  • It is difficult to deny that…
  • On balance, it seems that…
  • A reasonable conclusion is that…
